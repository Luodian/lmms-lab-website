## Abstract

Video generation models are increasingly fine-tuned to solve visual reasoning tasks by rendering the solution as a video. We ask how this approach compares, in accuracy and inference cost, with a similarly sized language model that reads the first frame and answers in text. On VBVR-Pro-Bench, we pair two released VBVR-Pro video models, a 5B and a 27B-total (14B-active) Wan2.2, with zero-shot Qwen3.5-4B and Qwen3.6-27B, which describe the scene as a JSON layout before answering. On one GPU with a dense BF16 peak of 989 TFLOP/s, a text answer requires <b className="sp-n-lang">364×</b> (5B) and <b className="sp-n-lang">786×</b> (27B) less GPU time with batching, and <b className="sp-n-lang">8.2×</b> and <b className="sp-n-lang">16.0×</b> less without. With the layout prompt, the two families show no significant accuracy difference under the strict success threshold (text − video: +5.5 and +4.8 percentage points), whereas under a lenient threshold that also accepts near-miss videos, the video models lead by 9.7 and 13.3 points. The two families succeed on complementary samples: at 27B, at least one of them solves 68% of the samples, yet only 89 of 475 are solved by both. Exploratory analyses at 27B suggest that, under the strict threshold, language models are stronger on symbolic answers and video models on single-agent path finding; under the lenient threshold, video models are also stronger on pixel-position answers. The comparison is not matched in training: the language models are zero-shot and graded on the final answer, whereas the video models are fine-tuned on VBVR-Pro data and graded on the entire video with partial credit.

## 1 Introduction

Recent work has shown that video generation models can solve visual reasoning tasks by generating the solution as a video \[[1](#ref-veo3), [2](#ref-vbvr), [3](#ref-vbvrpro)\]. VBVR-Pro, for instance, fine-tunes open video models on procedurally generated reasoning tasks and evaluates the generated videos with task-specific, rule-based scorers that track objects across frames. Many of these tasks, however, can also be solved symbolically. This raises two questions. At a matched parameter count, how much does it cost to reason about such tasks in a symbolic language, such as a written scene layout, a sequence of symbolic states, or code, and to report only the final answer? And how does the resulting accuracy compare with that of video generation? Since neither VBVR paper evaluates a text, symbolic, or code-based solver, both questions remain open.

The two approaches differ sharply in computational cost. A video model answers by propagating tens of thousands of latent tokens through dozens of denoising steps, whereas a language model reads the first frame and generates a few hundred to a few thousand tokens. The compute gap is therefore large by construction; whether rendering the solution yields a commensurate gain in accuracy is an empirical question.

In this work, we pair two released VBVR-Pro checkpoints, a 5B model and a 27B-total, 14B-active mixture-of-experts model, with zero-shot Qwen language models of similar size and evaluate both on VBVR-Pro-Bench. The language models receive the same first frame, at the resolution at which the video models generate. We grade their final answers against answer specifications frozen before grading, grade the videos with the benchmark's own v2 scorer, and measure the inference cost of every model on the same type of GPU, with a dense BF16 peak of 989 TFLOP/s.

Our contributions are as follows:

- A paired protocol for comparing text and video reasoners on identical benchmark samples, comprising answer specifications frozen before grading and validated against ground truth, a calibration of the video scorer on ground-truth videos, and a reproduction of the published video scores ([Appendix A](#a-protocol-alignment-with-vbvr-pro)).
- Measurements of GPU time per answer on identical hardware, both at batch size 1 and with batching, complemented by analytic forward FLOPs ([Figure 1](#fig-cost), [Appendix B](#b-computation-of-forward-flops)).
- An analysis of where the two modes of reasoning differ: by task class and answer kind, by which samples each solves, and by failure mode, with side-by-side outputs of both ([Figure 2](#fig-groups), [Figure 3](#fig-agree), [Figure 4](#fig-fail), [Figure 5](#fig-explorer)).
- A reproducible analysis in which all reported numbers are generated programmatically from the per-sample records ([Appendix F](#f-reproducibility)).

The principal caveat concerns training: the language models are zero-shot and graded on the final answer, whereas the video models are fine-tuned on VBVR-Pro data and graded on the entire video with partial credit. The comparison is therefore not matched in training ([Section 5](#5-limitations)).

## 2 Related work

**Video models as reasoners.** Video models can solve some perception and reasoning tasks zero-shot by generating the solution \[[1](#ref-veo3)\]. VBVR and VBVR-Pro construct large procedural task suites, fine-tune open video models on them, and score the videos with rule-based scorers \[[2](#ref-vbvr), [3](#ref-vbvrpro)\]. Other benchmarks evaluate video models on mazes, puzzles, and games \[[4](#ref-vrbench), [5](#ref-mmgr)\]. Scoring the entire process of a video answer, rather than only its final state, substantially lowers video scores \[[6](#ref-viper)\]. The VBVR-Pro models evaluated here build on the Wan family of open video models \[[7](#ref-wan)\].

**Reasoning in symbols and in pixels.** Several works interleave images with text during reasoning, such as visual thoughts drawn between reasoning steps \[[8](#ref-mvot)\] and plans composed of imagined images \[[9](#ref-visualplanning)\]. When a video model and a language model are fine-tuned on the same data, the video model performs better on mazes and on paths read from pixels \[[10](#ref-thinkframes), [4](#ref-vrbench)\], and visual world models help on geometry and physics \[[11](#ref-visworld)\]. On grid tasks, language models frequently write incorrect coordinates into the scene they describe, and these perception errors account for much of their failure \[[8](#ref-mvot), [9](#ref-visualplanning)\]. Text reasoners perform better on multi-object discrete puzzles such as Sokoban and Sudoku \[[4](#ref-vrbench), [12](#ref-motif), [5](#ref-mmgr)\]. Distilling visual thoughts into a text-only student can retain accuracy at a fraction of the wall-clock time \[[13](#ref-visualopsd)\].

**Representation on ARC-AGI.** On ARC-AGI, a video diffusion model outperformed a language model of similar size when neither had access to ARC training data \[[14](#ref-arcpixels)\]. With the same model, program induction and direct transduction solve largely different ARC tasks \[[15](#ref-barc)\]. Zero-shot video models solve almost none of the ARC-AGI-2 tasks \[[16](#ref-arcvideo)\].

**Code world models and cost.** Code world models learn rule-governed environments from few samples and simulate them far faster than a language model \[[17](#ref-poeworld), [18](#ref-gifmcts)\], and they outperform direct text-based play on ARC-AGI-3 at a matched budget \[[19](#ref-tycho)\]. One study reported large time and cost gaps between a video generator and a vision-language model of different sizes \[[20](#ref-collabvr)\]. To our knowledge, no prior work pairs size-matched text and video reasoners on the same benchmark samples and scorer while measuring cost on the same hardware.

### 3.1 Benchmark, answer specifications, and scorer calibration

VBVR-Pro-Bench comprises 100 tasks, 50 in-domain and 50 out-of-domain, with 5 samples each (500 samples in total). In-domain tasks are among VBVR-Pro's training tasks; out-of-domain tasks are not. Each sample consists of a first frame (1024×1024 for most tasks), a text prompt stating the task, a ground-truth solution video, and a metadata file with the semantic ground truth, i.e., the answer that the solution video depicts.

The v2 scorer (the VBVR-Pro-Bench evaluator at commit 95265d0, task-specific scorers only) reads the frames of a predicted video with OpenCV (color masks, contours, and object tracking) and returns a score between 0 and 1. Before grading any model, we froze, for 97 tasks, the metadata field that holds the final answer, the answer format given to the language model, and a comparator (exact match, a pixel tolerance, set match, or path validity on the grid). Each specification passed a known-answer check on all 5 samples: the ground-truth answer is graded correct, and a plausible incorrect answer is graded incorrect. Three tasks have no gradable final answer (two are pure animations, and in one the metadata contradicts the frames) and are excluded.

We scored the 500 ground-truth videos with the same v2 scorer and removed two tasks: one whose ground-truth videos do not all reach a score of 0.9, and one in which a video that does nothing receives full marks. The comparison uses the remaining 95 tasks (47 in-domain, 48 out-of-domain; 475 samples). Before observing any result, we fixed four task classes: selection or marking (the answer is an index, a label, a count, or a point), multi-object discrete (an order or configuration of several objects), single-agent path (one agent's path through a grid, maze, or graph), and continuous (geometry and physics). We additionally split tasks by answer kind: answers that contain pixel positions, and all other answers.

### 3.2 Video reasoners

G5 denotes VBVR-Pro-Wan2.2-TI2V-5B (5B parameters), and G27 denotes VBVR-Pro-Wan2.2-I2V-A14B (27B parameters in total: a mixture of two 14B experts, one of which is active at each denoising step). Both are Wan2.2 models that the VBVR-Pro authors fine-tuned with rank-32 LoRA on VBVR-Pro-SFT (1.25M samples over 250 tasks); we use the released checkpoints ([Table 1](#tab-models)).

Each model generates the solution video from the first frame and the task prompt at 512×512 resolution, with 81 frames, 50 denoising steps, and a classifier-free guidance scale of 5 (two network passes per step). The in-domain G5 videos use VBVR-Pro's own evaluation settings (seed and negative prompt); the remaining videos use our initial settings with the same resolution, length, number of steps, and guidance. Before scoring, VBVR-Pro's preparation script resizes each video to the 1024 canvas and truncates it to at most 5 seconds.

### 3.3 Text reasoners

We pair G5 with Qwen3.5-4B and G27 with Qwen3.6-27B, and include Qwen3.5-9B as an unpaired reference ([Table 1](#tab-models)). The language models are zero-shot and never see VBVR data. Each receives the first frame resized to 512×512, the task prompt, a reasoning instruction (the prompting strategy), and the answer format from the specification.

We use four prompting strategies: direct (state only the final answer), layout (first describe the scene as a JSON layout of the relevant objects, with shape, color, pixel position, and size, then reason over it, then answer), states (write the initial state and each subsequent state symbolically), and code (write Python that computes the answer; a sandbox executes it, and its output is taken as the answer). The main results use layout and direct; states and code appear in the ablations ([Section 4.7](#47-ablations)). We decode with vLLM 0.29.0 in BF16, greedily (temperature 0), with thinking mode disabled and at most 2,048 new tokens. The answer is the JSON object on the last line that begins with ANSWER. Pixel values in an answer are rescaled from the 512×512 frame to the 1024 canvas before grading.

### 3.4 Grading and statistical analysis

A text answer counts as solved if it passes the specification's comparator. A video counts as solved if its v2 score is at least 0.9, the strict threshold fixed before any result was observed. A threshold of 0.7, fixed at the same time, serves as a sensitivity analysis, because the v2 scorers also grade the process (e.g., how the agent moves), whereas the text grader checks only the final answer.

Rates are averaged over tasks, and differences are reported as text − video in percentage points (pp), paired by sample. We compute 95% confidence intervals (CIs) from 4,000 bootstrap resamples over tasks (seed 0), shown in brackets, and refer to a difference as significant when its CI excludes zero. The analyses by task class and answer kind are exploratory: the class-level predictions specified before the study concern a fine-tuned language model, which this paper does not evaluate, and significance in these analyses is not corrected for multiple comparisons.

### 3.5 Cost measurement

All measurements were performed on one GPU of the same type, with a dense BF16 peak of 989 TFLOP/s. For text, "batched" runs all 485 requests of one prompting strategy in a single vLLM batch, and "batch size 1" sends one sample per task (97 requests) sequentially with prefix caching disabled. For video, we record the generation time of each video, generated one at a time. We also compute analytic forward FLOPs per answer: for video, the DiT forward pass over the latent tokens times 100 passes (50 steps, two passes each); for text, the vision encoder plus the language model over the prompt and the generated tokens ([Figure 1](#fig-cost), [Appendix B](#b-computation-of-forward-flops)).

## 4 Results

### 4.1 Inference cost per answer

Textual reasoning is substantially cheaper than video generation at both model sizes ([Table 2](#tab-main), [Table 3](#tab-ratio), [Figure 1](#fig-cost)). With batched inference, a text answer takes <b className="sp-n-lang">0.061</b> s of GPU time for Qwen3.5-4B and <b className="sp-n-lang">0.309</b> s for Qwen3.6-27B (layout prompt), whereas generating a single video takes <b className="sp-n-video">22.0</b> s with G5 and <b className="sp-n-video">243</b> s with G27, corresponding to <b className="sp-n-lang">364×</b> and <b className="sp-n-lang">786×</b> less GPU time. Without batching, the gap narrows to 8.2× and 16.0×, because a single decoding stream is memory-bound: each new token requires reading all model weights, so the GPU operates at only 0.4% to 0.8% of its dense BF16 peak. Batching 485 requests raises this utilization to 20% to 33%. The video models, in contrast, already reach 27% (G5) and 38% (G27) of peak when generating a single video, so batching could accelerate them by at most 3.7× and 2.7×; even at full utilization, they would remain 97× and 295× slower than batched text inference. The analytic forward FLOPs, which differ by 480× and 1,026× per answer, corroborate the magnitude of the gap ([Appendix B](#b-computation-of-forward-flops)), and the gap persists when cost is normalized by accuracy: per solved answer, batched text inference is 453× and 878× cheaper under the strict threshold and 270× and 609× cheaper under the lenient one.

### 4.2 Accuracy at matched scale

With the layout prompt, the zero-shot language models show no significant difference from the fine-tuned video models under the strict threshold ([Table 3](#tab-ratio)). Qwen3.5-4B attains a solve rate of <b className="sp-n-lang">0.278</b>, compared with <b className="sp-n-video">0.223</b> for G5, and Qwen3.6-27B attains <b className="sp-n-lang">0.459</b>, compared with <b className="sp-n-video">0.410</b> for G27, corresponding to differences of +5.5 and +4.8 pp. Relaxing the threshold to 0.7 shifts the comparison in favor of the video models, which then achieve higher solve rates by 9.7 pp at 5B and 13.3 pp at 27B; only the latter difference is significant. This shift is concentrated on the in-domain tasks encountered during fine-tuning: at 27B, the difference under the lenient threshold is −27.2 pp on in-domain tasks but +0.4 pp on out-of-domain tasks. Direct answering yields slightly higher solve rates than layout prompting (0.331 at 4B and 0.503 at 27B), although these gains fall within the CIs; with direct answering, the 4B model outperforms G5 significantly under the strict threshold (+10.7 pp), the only significant difference for this prompt. [Section 4.7](#47-ablations) compares all four prompting strategies.

### 4.3 Where symbolic and visual reasoning differ

[Figure 2](#fig-groups) decomposes the primary comparison (27B, layout prompt, strict threshold) by split, task class, and answer kind, and [Table 4](#tab-groups) reports both pairs under both thresholds. The clearest pattern emerges along answer kind. When the answer is symbolic rather than a pixel position, such as an option, a count, an order, a value, or a list of grid cells, the language models significantly outperform the video models under the strict threshold at both scales, by <b className="sp-n-lang">18.8</b> pp at 27B and <b className="sp-n-lang">20.8</b> pp at 5B. Conversely, when the answer is a pixel position, the advantage shifts to the video models: at 27B, the difference of <b className="sp-n-video">10.7</b> pp in their favor under the strict threshold is not significant, but it grows to a significant <b className="sp-n-video">26.7</b> pp under the lenient threshold. Among the task classes, the video models significantly outperform the language models on single-agent path finding, by <b className="sp-n-video">29.1</b> pp at 27B under the strict threshold; this class, however, comprises only 11 tasks, 9 of which are in-domain. The remaining three classes show no significant difference in this configuration. Part of the pixel-answer gap is attributable to the matched 512² input, since the language models perform substantially better on pixel answers at the native 1024² resolution ([Section 4.7](#47-ablations)). As none of these comparisons was specified in advance as a hypothesis test, we regard them as exploratory.

### 4.4 Complementary successes

Beyond aggregate accuracy, the two families succeed on markedly different samples ([Figure 3](#fig-agree)). At 27B, only 89 of the 475 paired samples are solved by both models, whereas <b className="sp-n-lang">129</b> are solved only by the language model and <b className="sp-n-video">106</b> only by the video model. Consequently, the union of the two models attains a solve rate of 0.682, compared with 0.459 for the better model alone; at 5B, the corresponding values are 0.438 and 0.278. Although this union is an oracle upper bound, since no mechanism selects the appropriate model for each sample, it indicates substantial complementarity between the two approaches. The division again follows the answer kind: on non-pixel answers, 85 samples are solved only by the language model and 38 only by the video model, whereas on pixel answers the corresponding counts are 44 and 68.

### 4.5 Failure modes

The two families also fail in qualitatively different ways ([Figure 4](#fig-fail)). With the layout prompt, 139 of Qwen3.6-27B's 475 answers (29%) are parsed but incorrect, and a further 118 (25%) exhaust the 2,048-token budget before reaching the answer line; at 4B, another 58 answers (12%) terminate without a parsable answer. Direct answering shortens the outputs, yet 93 of Qwen3.6-27B's direct answers still reach the limit. The video scores, in contrast, are bimodal: G27 scores below 0.1 on 73 samples and at least 0.9 on 195, while 86 samples (18%) fall between 0.7 and 0.9. These near misses count as solved only under the lenient threshold, and because the v2 scorers award partial credit for the process, this band reflects properties of the scorer as well as of the reasoning.

### 4.7 Ablations

**Prompting strategies.** [Table 5](#tab-hints) compares the four prompting strategies on the earlier runs with 512² input (97 tasks, final answers only; Qwen3.6-27B served via OpenRouter). No single strategy is best across model sizes: direct answering performs best at 4B, and code at 9B and 27B. At 4B and 27B, direct answering lies within the CI of the best strategy, whereas at 9B, code attains a solve rate of 0.390, which lies outside the CI of direct answering (0.305). Because these CIs are unpaired, they bound the differences only approximately.

**Inference backend.** The timed runs closely reproduce the earlier ones. With the layout prompt, Qwen3.5-4B attains a solve rate of 0.278 in the timed runs versus 0.267 in the earlier runs, with 450 of 475 per-sample verdicts in agreement, and Qwen3.5-9B attains 0.349 versus 0.335 (440 in agreement). For Qwen3.6-27B, which was previously served via OpenRouter, the solve rate is 0.459 on vLLM in BF16 versus 0.490 via OpenRouter (412 in agreement), a backend difference of 3.2 pp.

**Input resolution.** Downscaling the frame from 1024² to the 512² input used by the video models substantially reduces the language models' accuracy on the 46 pixel-answer tasks, from 0.261 to 0.166 for Qwen3.5-4B, from 0.350 to 0.180 for Qwen3.5-9B, and from 0.560 to 0.389 for Qwen3.6-27B (pooled over prompting strategies), whereas the remaining 51 tasks change by at most 1.9 pp. The matched comparison therefore retains the 512² input at some cost to the language models' pixel accuracy.

## 5 Limitations

- **Training.** The language models are evaluated zero-shot, whereas G5 and G27 were fine-tuned on VBVR-Pro-SFT (1.25M samples over 250 tasks). Since no language model was fine-tuned on the same data, the comparison is not matched in training.
- **Grading.** The v2 scorer grades the entire video with partial credit and also assesses the process, whereas a text answer is graded as either correct or incorrect, with the answer format specified in its prompt. The two thresholds (0.9 and 0.7) bracket this asymmetry but cannot remove it.
- **Reproduction.** G27 was run with our initial sampling settings and scores 1.8 points below its model card overall and 3.5 points below on in-domain tasks; the 27B comparison may therefore favor the language model by a few pp on in-domain tasks ([Appendix A](#a-protocol-alignment-with-vbvr-pro)).
- **Benchmark overlap.** Of the 235 in-domain benchmark samples (47 tasks checked), 168 have an identical counterpart in VBVR-Pro-SFT. G5 scores no higher on these duplicates (−0.007 v2 mean) than on the other samples of the same tasks, and the language models never saw them.
- **Exploratory analyses.** The task classes were pre-specified for a fine-tuned language model, and the answer-kind split was defined after the zero-shot runs; moreover, the single-agent path class contains only 11 tasks.
- **Decoding.** We perform a single greedy run per model and prompting strategy, and answers exceeding 2,048 tokens are counted as incorrect (25% of Qwen3.6-27B's layout answers). The 27B result shifts by 3.2 pp between two serving backends.
- **Cost.** All runs used one GPU of the same type. Because the video models were run one video at a time, their batched cost is bounded rather than measured, and the batched text time includes waiting for the longest answers. Video FLOPs are derived from the model shapes and text FLOPs from the measured token counts ([Appendix B](#b-computation-of-forward-flops)).
- **Pretraining.** Pretraining compute is not matched. Public data suggest that the language models received similar or greater pretraining compute, although both estimates are approximate ([Appendix C](#c-estimated-pretraining-compute)).

## 6 Conclusion

We compared two ways of solving visual reasoning tasks at matched model size on VBVR-Pro-Bench: rendering the solution with a fine-tuned video model, and reasoning in text with a zero-shot language model. With the layout prompt, the two approaches show no significant difference in accuracy under the strict threshold, while textual reasoning requires two to three orders of magnitude less GPU time per answer with batching and about one order of magnitude less without it. The lenient threshold favors the video models, mainly on the in-domain tasks on which they were trained. The two approaches also succeed on complementary samples: exploratory analyses suggest that, under the strict threshold, the language models are stronger when the answer is symbolic and the video models on single-agent path finding at 27B, and that under the lenient threshold, the video models are also stronger on pixel-position answers. Finally, their failure modes differ: many language-model failures stem from exceeding the decoding budget, whereas the video scores separate into clear failures and near misses.

Two directions follow from these findings. The first is the matched comparison that this study does not make: fine-tuning the language model on the same VBVR-Pro training samples as the video model. The second is to combine the two approaches, letting text decide and pixels localize, and to test whether such a combination retains the strengths of both.

### C Estimated pretraining compute

All figures are derived from public data. Qwen3.5/3.6 and Wan2.1/2.2 do not disclose their training compute, so their rows are estimates based on the assumptions listed in the table.

- Language models: $C = 6 \cdot N \cdot D$, where N counts the weights involved in matrix multiplications (layers, output head, and MTP) and D the number of pretraining tokens. Qwen3.5/3.6 do not state D, so Qwen3's 36T tokens are assumed. This is not a lower bound: the blog's "significantly larger" refers to visual-text tokens, Qwen3-Next was trained on a 15T subset, and the new tokenizer packs more text into each token.
- Video models: disclosed GPU-hours × 989 TFLOP/s (dense BF16) × the disclosed MFU for Seaweed-7B and Open-Sora 2.0; samples seen per stage × training FLOPs per sample for Movie Gen and Step-Video-T2V; tokens × FLOPs per token for Wan, whose paper states only "O(1) trillions of tokens" (interpreted as 1–5T).
- Wan2.2 is extrapolated from Wan2.1 using its stated data growth (+65.6% images, +83.2% videos); for TI2V-5B, we assume A14B's samples at 4× fewer tokens per sample.
- The language models likely received more pretraining compute, as they are trained on tens of trillions of tokens, whereas the video models see O(1)T tokens, each of which is more expensive because video sequences are long. Seaweed-7B, the only video model that discloses its from-scratch compute, used 9.0×10<sup>23</sup> FLOPs, so the Wan-based ranges may be underestimates.
- VBVR fine-tuning is small by comparison: VBVR-Pro's RL run on G5 used 128 H800 GPUs for 5.1 days, about 15.7k GPU-hours.

### F Reproducibility

All numbers in this report are generated programmatically from the per-sample records.

An earlier build of this report retained G-161 in the paired set because the exclusion list referred to the task with an incorrect suffix, so 96 tasks were paired instead of 95. Since a video that does nothing passes G-161's scorer, the protocol excludes this task from every comparison. The correction changed the overall paired differences by at most 0.9 pp and the in-domain and out-of-domain differences by at most 1.4 pp; no conclusion changed.

The per-sample records comprise:

- the frozen answer specifications for the 97 gradable tasks;
- the v2 scores of G5, G27 and the ground-truth videos, with the generation time of each video;
- the raw text outputs and grades of every model and prompting strategy, from the vLLM and OpenRouter runs;
- the measured cost per answer and the paired tables at both thresholds; and
- the per-group differences, agreement statistics, failure categories, and the example set.
